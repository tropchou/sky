=== [00:39] 야 멍청이들 ===
meaning: "Hey, you idiots."
tone: Casual, playful/affectionate. Hwasa addressing her fans (Twits) in a teasing, familiar way.

--- word ---
term: 야
type: interjection
breakdown:
meaning: "Hey" / "Yo"
notes: A casual attention-getter used only with people you're close to or in 반말 (informal speech). It would be rude to use with strangers or elders. In the context of a Kpop idol addressing their fandom, it signals closeness and a relaxed, unfiltered vibe — Hwasa is known for this kind of direct, no-filter communication style. The polite equivalents would be 여러분 ("everyone") or 저기요 ("excuse me"), but those would completely change the tone here.

--- word ---
term: 멍청이들
type: noun (plural)
breakdown: 멍청이 ("idiot/fool") + 들 (plural particle)
meaning: "idiots" / "fools"
notes: 멍청이 is a common casual insult meaning "idiot" or "dummy." It comes from the adjective 멍청하다 ("to be stupid/dim-witted"). The suffix -이 turns the adjective root into a noun referring to a person (similar pattern: 뚱뚱하다 → 뚱뚱이, "fatty"). 들 marks it as plural — she's addressing a group (her fans). In this context it's 100% affectionate, not actually insulting. Korean fandoms and idols often use playful insults as terms of endearment — it's a sign of comfort and closeness, similar to calling your friends "you dummies" in English. Outside of this kind of relationship, calling someone 멍청이 would genuinely offend.


=== [00:46] 난 이제 자야지 ===
meaning: "I should go to sleep now."
tone: Informal, self-directed resolution. Hwasa casually telling her Twits she's wrapping up and heading to bed.

--- word ---
term: 난
type: pronoun + particle
breakdown: 나 ("I") + 는 (topic particle)
meaning: "I" (as topic)
notes: Contraction of 나는. Very common in casual speech and texting. You'll almost never hear 나는 in fast everyday conversation — 난 is the natural form. Similar contractions: 넌 (너는, "you" — casual), 전 (저는, "I" — humble/polite form). Since Hwasa is speaking in 반말 (casual speech) to her fans, 난 fits naturally here. If she were on a formal broadcast she might say 저는 이제 자야 해요.

--- word ---
term: 이제
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "now" / "from now on"
notes: Implies a shift or transition — "now (that things have changed)" or "from this point." Different from 지금 ("right now"), which is neutral and time-specific. 이제 often carries a sense of finality or decision, which is why it pairs well with 자야지 here — she's signaling "okay, that's it, time for bed." Compare: 지금 자 ("sleep right now") vs. 이제 자야지 ("I should sleep now" — I've made the call, the moment has come). Very natural way to wind down a late-night fan stream.

--- word ---
term: 자야지
type: verb ending
breakdown: 자다 ("to sleep") + -아/어야지
meaning: "I should sleep" / "I'd better sleep"
notes: -아/어야지 is the casual, spoken form of -아/어야 하다 ("have to / must"). The -지 ending softens it into a personal resolve rather than a strict obligation. Very common in self-talk: 먹어야지 ("I should eat"), 가야지 ("I should go"). When directed at someone else, it can sound nagging: 너도 자야지 ("you should sleep too"). Textbook Korean would use -아/어야 해요 or -아/어야 돼요 ("have to / should"), but in real life -아/어야지 is far more natural for self-directed statements. Here it gives a warm, winding-down-the-stream feeling — like she's thinking out loud to her Twits rather than making a formal announcement.


=== [00:53] 뭘 왜야 ===
meaning: "What? Why?"
tone: Very casual, slightly dismissive or playfully confused. Hwasa reacting to something her Twits said or did.

--- word ---
term: 뭘
type: pronoun + particle
breakdown: 뭐 ("what") + 를 (object particle)
meaning: "what" (as object)
notes: Contraction of 뭐를. Extremely common in spoken Korean — you'll rarely hear 뭐를 said in full. 뭐 itself is already a casual contraction of 무엇 ("what," formal/written). So the chain is: 무엇을 → 뭐를 → 뭘. Here it's used in a clipped, reactionary way — she's not forming a full sentence, just firing back "what (are you talking about)?" This kind of sentence fragment is very natural in Korean conversation, especially in 반말 (casual speech). Similar contractions: 걸 (것을, "the thing" + object particle), 날 (나를, "me" + object particle).

--- word ---
term: 왜야
type: adverb + copula ending
breakdown: 왜 ("why") + -야 (casual copula ending of 이다, "to be")
meaning: "why?" / "what's with that?"
notes: 왜 alone means "why," but adding -야 turns it into something closer to "why is it?" or "what's the deal?" It's a very colloquial, slightly exasperated expression. The -야 here is the 반말 (casual) form of the copula 이다 ("to be") — polite version would be 왜예요 or 왜요. 왜야 has a punchy, almost confrontational feel but in this fan context it's playful — like "what, why?" when someone says something unexpected. You'll hear this a lot in casual Korean conversations and variety shows. Compare: 왜 ("why" — neutral), 왜요 ("why" — polite), 왜야 ("why?" — casual, with a bit of attitude).


=== [00:56] 왜 다 왜 왔냐는 반응이야 ===
meaning: "Why is everyone's reaction like 'why did you come?'"
tone: Casual, amused/mock-offended. Hwasa teasing her Twits for acting surprised or unwelcoming that she showed up.

--- word ---
term: 왜
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "why"
notes: Appears twice in this sentence with different functions. The first 왜 is Hwasa's own "why" — she's questioning her fans' reaction. The second 왜 is part of the quoted phrase 왜 왔냐 ("why did you come?"), which is what the fans are supposedly saying. This kind of repetition is very natural in Korean when you're quoting someone else's words that also contain a question. It can sound confusing at first, but context makes it clear.

--- word ---
term: 다
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "all" / "everyone"
notes: 다 means "all" or "everything." Here it refers to everyone in the chat — "why is everyone (reacting this way)?" It's a very high-frequency word. Compare: 다 먹었어 ("I ate it all"), 다 왔어 ("everyone's here"). Short, simple, but essential.

--- word ---
term: 왜 왔냐는
type: quoted clause + quoting particle
breakdown: 왜 ("why") + 오다 ("to come") + -았- (past tense) + -냐 (casual question ending) + -는 (quoting/modifying particle)
meaning: "(saying) 'why did you come?'"
notes: This is indirect quotation, one of the trickier Korean grammar points. The structure is: a quoted question (왜 왔냐, "why did you come?") + -는, which turns the whole quote into a modifier for the next noun (반응, "reaction"). The full underlying form is 왜 왔냐고 하는, but in spoken Korean -고 하 gets dropped, leaving just -냐는. This contraction is extremely common. -냐 is a casual question ending used in 반말 — the polite equivalent would be 왜 왔느냐 or 왜 왔냐고요. Learners often struggle with quoted speech — the key pattern to memorize is: [quoted sentence] + -고 하다 ("to say that..."), and know that in casual speech it almost always gets shortened.

--- word ---
term: 반응이야
type: noun + copula
breakdown: 반응 ("reaction/response") + 이다 ("to be") in casual form → 이야
meaning: "it's (the) reaction" / "the reaction is"
notes: 반응 is a Sino-Korean word (from Chinese 反應) meaning "reaction" or "response." Very commonly used when talking about how people react to something, especially online — 반응이 좋다 ("the reaction is good"), 반응이 뜨겁다 ("the reaction is heated/intense"). The ending 이야 is the 반말 (casual) copula form of 이다 ("to be"), used after nouns ending in a consonant. After a vowel it becomes -야 (e.g., 뭐야, "what is it?"). Polite version would be 반응이에요. Here Hwasa is wrapping up her observation: the reaction is (one of) "why did you come?" — said with playful disbelief.

=== [01:03] 무슨 반응인 거야 이거는 ===
meaning: "What kind of reaction is this?"
tone: Casual, mock-indignant. Hwasa continuing to playfully call out her Twits' unwelcoming response.

--- word ---
term: 무슨
type: determiner
breakdown:
meaning: "what kind of" / "what"
notes: 무슨 is used before a noun to ask "what kind of" or to express disbelief — similar to English "what (kind of)...?" Compare with 뭐 ("what" — stands alone as a pronoun) and 어떤 ("which/what kind of" — more neutral). 무슨 often carries a tone of surprise or incredulity: 무슨 소리야 ("what are you talking about?" — literally "what sound is that?"), 무슨 일이야 ("what's going on?"). Here it's rhetorical — she's not genuinely asking, she's expressing "I can't believe this reaction."

--- word ---
term: 반응인
type: noun + copula (modifying form)
breakdown: 반응 ("reaction") + 이다 ("to be") → modifier form 인
meaning: "reaction that is" / "(which) is a reaction"
notes: 인 is the modifying (관형형, gwanhyeonghyeong) form of the copula 이다 ("to be"). It lets you attach 이다 to a following noun as a descriptor. Here 반응인 modifies 거 — so 반응인 거 means "a thing that is a reaction." The full uncontracted form is 반응인 것, but 것 → 거 is standard in spoken Korean. This pattern [noun + 인 + 거] is extremely common: 학생인 거야 ("it's that (they) are a student"), 진짜인 거야 ("it's that it's real").

--- word ---
term: 거야
type: noun (dependent) + copula
breakdown: 것 ("thing/fact") → 거 (spoken contraction) + 이다 ("to be") → 야 (casual ending)
meaning: "is it?" / "it is"
notes: 거 is the spoken contraction of 것 ("thing"). 것 is a dependent noun used constantly in Korean to nominalize or wrap up a clause — think of it as "the fact that" or "the thing." -야 is the 반말 (casual) copula after a vowel. Together, 거야 is one of the most common casual sentence endings you'll hear. It can be a statement (그런 거야, "that's how it is") or a question (뭔 거야, "what is it?") depending on intonation. Polite version: 거예요. Learners should get very comfortable with this pattern early — it's everywhere.

--- word ---
term: 이거는
type: pronoun + particle
breakdown: 이것 ("this thing") → 이거 (spoken contraction) + 는 (topic particle)
meaning: "this (as for this)"
notes: 이거 is the casual spoken form of 이것 ("this thing"). Adding 는 (topic particle) emphasizes contrast or singles it out — "as for THIS..." It's placed at the end of the sentence here, which is a very natural Korean spoken word order shift called 도치 (inversion/afterthought). In textbook order it would be 이거는 무슨 반응인 거야, but putting 이거는 at the end adds a feeling of "...I mean, seriously, THIS?" — like she's tacking it on as an exasperated afterthought. This kind of rearrangement is extremely common in casual spoken Korean and something textbooks rarely teach.


=== [01:07] 갑작스럽긴 하지 ===
meaning: It is kind of sudden, though.
tone: Informal, reflective, self-acknowledging. The speaker is admitting something is sudden, almost talking to themselves or softly agreeing with an unspoken thought.

--- word ---
term: 갑작스럽긴
type: adjective + contraction
breakdown: 갑작스럽다 (to be sudden) → 갑작스럽 (stem) + 기는 (기는 contracted to 긴)
meaning: being sudden (with concessive nuance)
notes: 갑작스럽다 is the descriptive verb meaning "to be sudden/abrupt." It is built from the noun 갑작 (suddenness) + the suffix -스럽다, which turns nouns into adjectives meaning "having the quality of." Other examples: 사랑스럽다 (to be lovely, from 사랑 love), 자연스럽다 (to be natural, from 자연 nature). The ending -기는 (nominalization + topic particle 는) is used to concede a point: "it IS [X], but..." In casual speech -기는 almost always contracts to -긴. The full uncontracted form 갑작스럽기는 would sound stiff and overly formal in conversation. Learners should get comfortable recognizing -긴 as this contraction since it appears constantly in spoken Korean.

--- word ---
term: 하지
type: auxiliary verb + casual ending
breakdown: 하다 (to do/to be) → 하 (stem) + 지 (sentence-ending particle)
meaning: it is, right / though
notes: Here 하다 functions as an auxiliary verb completing the -긴 하다 pattern. The full construction -긴 하다 means "it IS (something), admittedly" — it acknowledges a fact while implying there is more to the story. The ending -지 in this position is a soft sentence ender that seeks mild agreement or expresses self-confirmation, similar to "right?" or "though" in English. It is 반말 (casual speech), so it would only be used with friends, people younger than you, or when talking to yourself. Compare: -지요/죠 is the polite equivalent. Note that -지 has many uses in Korean — it can mark negation (않지), rhetorical questions, or soft assertions depending on context. Here it gives a musing, reflective feel, like the speaker is acknowledging reality to themselves.

=== [01:09] 나 원래 갑작스럽게 오잖아 ===
meaning: I always come suddenly, you know.
tone: Informal, playful, self-justifying. The speaker is defending the suddenness by reminding listeners this is just how they are. Feels lighthearted and characteristic of idol-to-fan casual talk.

--- word ---
term: 나
type: pronoun
breakdown:
meaning: I / me
notes: 나 is the casual first-person pronoun, used in 반말 (informal speech). The polite equivalent is 저. Here it appears without any particle — in spoken Korean, the subject particle 가 (나 + 가 → 내가) is very frequently dropped. Learners should note that particle-dropping is normal and expected in casual speech, not a mistake.

--- word ---
term: 원래
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: originally / by nature / always (as a general trait)
notes: 원래 means "originally" or "by nature." It is used to describe something that has always been the case or is someone's inherent characteristic. Here it carries the nuance of "I've always been this way" — it is not about a single past event but a general pattern. Common in everyday speech. Compare with 항상 (always), which emphasizes frequency, whereas 원래 emphasizes that something is a fundamental or default trait. Example: 나 원래 이래 (I'm just like this by nature).

--- word ---
term: 갑작스럽게
type: adverb (derived from adjective)
breakdown: 갑작스럽다 (to be sudden) → 갑작스럽 (stem) + 게 (adverbial suffix)
meaning: suddenly / in a sudden manner
notes: The suffix -게 converts a descriptive verb (adjective) into an adverb, similar to adding "-ly" in English. 갑작스럽다 → 갑작스럽게 (suddenly). This is one of the most productive and useful patterns in Korean. Other examples: 자연스럽다 → 자연스럽게 (naturally), 조용하다 → 조용하게 (quietly). Note the simpler synonym 갑자기 also means "suddenly" and is more common in everyday speech. 갑작스럽게 feels slightly more descriptive or emphatic by comparison.

--- word ---
term: 오잖아
type: verb + ending
breakdown: 오다 (to come) → 오 (stem) + 잖아 (sentence ending)
meaning: I come, you know / as you know, I come
notes: -잖아 is a casual sentence ending that means "you know" or "as you already know." It reminds the listener of something both parties should be aware of. It is a contraction of -지 않다 (to not) but has evolved into its own distinct meaning — it no longer carries a negative sense. This is a common point of confusion for learners: -잖아 does NOT mean "don't" here. Full polite form: -잖아요. This ending is extremely common in spoken Korean, especially when someone is explaining themselves or lightly protesting. Here the speaker is saying "you already know I show up suddenly" — it is playful self-awareness. Related: -잖아 can sound slightly whiny or insistent depending on tone, but here it is lighthearted.


=== [01:16] 더 늦은 시간에도 오잖아 ===
meaning: I even come at later times, you know.
tone: Informal, playful, continuing to justify the suddenness. Hwasa is building on the previous point — not only does she come suddenly, she even comes at later hours than this. Casual idol-to-fan banter.

--- word ---
term: 더
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: more / even more
notes: 더 is one of the most common adverbs in Korean, meaning "more." It modifies the adjective 늦은 here, creating "later" (more late). Unlike English, Korean does not change the adjective form for comparatives — instead 더 is simply placed before the adjective. 더 크다 (to be bigger), 더 빠르다 (to be faster). Simple but essential word that learners should internalize early.

--- word ---
term: 늦은
type: adjective (modifier form)
breakdown: 늦다 (to be late) → 늦 (stem) + 은 (modifier/관형형 ending)
meaning: late
notes: The ending -(으)ㄴ is the past/present modifier form for descriptive verbs (adjectives), allowing them to modify a following noun. 늦다 → 늦은 시간 (a late time). For action verbs this same ending indicates past tense modification, but for descriptive verbs like 늦다 it indicates a present state. This is a key distinction that trips up learners. Compare: 먹은 사람 (a person who ate — past action) vs 늦은 시간 (a late time — present description).

--- word ---
term: 시간에도
type: noun + particles
breakdown: 시간 (time) + 에 (locative particle) + 도 (particle meaning "also/even")
meaning: at times, even / even at times
notes: 시간 means "time" or "hour." The particle 에 marks a point in time ("at"). The particle 도 adds the meaning of "also" or "even." When stacked together, 에도 means "even at." Particle stacking is very common in Korean — learners should get comfortable combining particles. The 도 here is what gives the sentence its "even" emphasis: the speaker is saying "I come even at later times than this." Other stacking examples: 에서도 (even at/from a place), 한테도 (even to a person).

--- word ---
term: 오잖아
type: verb + ending
breakdown: 오다 (to come) → 오 (stem) + 잖아 (sentence ending)
meaning: I come, you know
notes: Same construction as in the previous sentence [01:09]. The speaker is reusing -잖아 to again appeal to shared knowledge with the listener. Repetition of the same ending across sentences is natural in Korean conversation, especially when someone is making a continuing argument. Here it reinforces the tone of "come on, you already know this about me." See notes at [01:09] for full breakdown of -잖아.


=== [01:22] 고마워 ===
meaning: "Thank you." / "Thanks."
tone: Casual, warm. Hwasa sincerely thanking her Twits after the playful back-and-forth.

--- word ---
term: 고마워
type: adjective (conjugated)
breakdown: 고맙다 ("to be thankful/grateful") + -아/어 (casual ending) → 고마워
meaning: "thanks" / "thank you"
notes: 고맙다 is an irregular adjective — the ㅂ in the stem drops and becomes 우 when conjugated, giving 고마워 instead of the expected 고맙아. This ㅂ-irregular pattern is very common and trips up learners: 춥다 ("to be cold") → 추워, 어렵다 ("to be difficult") → 어려워, 귀엽다 ("to be cute") → 귀여워. 고마워 is 반말 (casual) — use it only with close friends, younger people, or in contexts like this where an idol is being familiar with fans. Polite versions: 고마워요 (casual-polite), 감사합니다 (formal-polite). 감사합니다 is Sino-Korean (from 感謝) and more appropriate in professional or formal settings. Between friends and fans, 고마워 feels genuine and intimate — using 감사합니다 here would create emotional distance.


=== [01:27] 왜 이렇게 다 놀래 ===
meaning: "Why is everyone so surprised?"
tone: Casual, amused. Hwasa still reacting to her Twits being shocked that she showed up.

--- word ---
term: 왜
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "why"
notes: Straightforward "why" here — Hwasa is genuinely (or playfully) questioning why her fans are so startled. No special usage beyond what was covered at [00:53]. High-frequency word that appears constantly in casual Korean conversation.

--- word ---
term: 이렇게
type: adverb
breakdown: 이렇다 ("to be like this") → 이렇게 (adverbial form)
meaning: "like this" / "this much" / "so"
notes: 이렇게 is the adverbial form of 이렇다, made by adding -게 (which turns adjectives/verbs into adverbs, similar to "-ly" in English). Here it functions as an intensifier — "why so (much)...?" / "why this much...?" This is part of a three-way set based on distance, just like this/that in English: 이렇게 ("like this" — near speaker), 그렇게 ("like that" — near listener or previously mentioned), 저렇게 ("like that over there" — far from both). Very useful pattern: 왜 이렇게 비싸 ("why is it so expensive?"), 왜 이렇게 추워 ("why is it so cold?"). The 왜 이렇게 combo is extremely common in everyday Korean for expressing exasperation or surprise.

--- word ---
term: 다
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "all" / "everyone"
notes: Same usage as [00:56] — referring to all the fans in the chat. "Why is everyone so surprised?" 다 is doing the heavy lifting of turning an individual action into a collective one.

--- word ---
term: 놀래
type: verb (conjugated)
breakdown: 놀라다 ("to be surprised/startled") → 놀래 (casual spoken form)
meaning: "be surprised" / "are (you) surprised"
notes: This is a common casual/colloquial contraction. The standard conjugation of 놀라다 would be 놀라 (with -아 ending), but in spoken Korean 놀래 is widely used instead. Technically 놀래 comes from 놀라다's causative form 놀래다 ("to surprise someone"), but in everyday speech people use 놀래 interchangeably to mean both "to be surprised" and "to surprise." This blurring is very common — purists might insist on 놀라 for "be surprised" vs. 놀래 for "surprise someone," but in real casual conversation like this, the distinction mostly disappears. Compare: 놀랐어 ("I was surprised" — past tense, standard), 깜짝 놀랐어 ("I was startled" — with the onomatopoeia 깜짝, "with a jump/shock"). Here Hwasa is asking why everyone is reacting with shock — the playful tone from the previous sentences continues.


=== [01:31] 너무 갑자기 라니 ===
meaning: "You're saying it's too sudden?" / "'Too sudden,' they say..."
tone: Casual, amused/teasing. Hwasa quoting or echoing what her Twits are saying in the chat and reacting to it.

--- word ---
term: 너무
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "too (much)" / "so" / "very"
notes: 너무 originally means "too much" (excessive, negative), but in modern casual Korean it's used just as often to mean "very" or "so" in a positive or neutral sense — 너무 예뻐 ("so pretty"), 너무 맛있어 ("so delicious"). Textbooks and older grammar guides may say 너무 should only be negative, but that ship has sailed in everyday speech. The "correct" positive alternative would be 아주 or 매우 ("very"), but these sound stiff in casual conversation. Here it keeps its original "too much" sense — the fans are saying the surprise was excessive.

--- word ---
term: 갑자기
type: adverb
breakdown:
meaning: "suddenly" / "all of a sudden"
notes: A very common standalone adverb with no conjugation needed. Used exactly like English "suddenly": 갑자기 비가 왔어 ("it suddenly rained"), 갑자기 왜? ("why all of a sudden?"). Here the fans are complaining that Hwasa appeared out of nowhere — 갑자기 captures that shock. No irregular forms or tricky grammar to worry about with this one. Good vocabulary to memorize early.

--- word ---
term: 라니
type: quoting ending
breakdown: -라 (quotation particle, from -라고, "saying that") + -니 (exclamatory/disbelief ending)
meaning: "...they say?" / "to say that...!" / "can you believe they're saying...?"
notes: -라니 is a spoken contraction of -라고 하니 ("to say that..."). It expresses surprise, disbelief, or mild indignation at what someone else said. Here Hwasa is echoing her fans' words back at them — they typed something like "너무 갑자기!" and she's repeating it with -라니 to express "can you believe they're saying 'too sudden'?!" The pattern is: [quoted word/phrase] + -라니. Examples: 못생겼다라니 ("to say (I'm) ugly...!"), 싫다라니 ("to say (you) don't like it...!"). After nouns (like here, after the adverb used as a quoted phrase), you use -라니. === [02:55] 보고 싶더라고 ===
meaning: I was missing you / I realized I wanted to see you.
tone: Informal, warm, sincere. Hwasa telling her Twits she missed them — a sweet moment in the middle of her rambling setup.

--- word ---
term: 보고
type: verb + connective
breakdown: 보다 (to see) → 보 (stem) + 고 (connective)
meaning: to see
notes: 보다 here means "to see (someone)" in the sense of meeting or being with them. 보고 connects to 싶다 to form the compound pattern 보고 싶다 (to want to see / to miss someone). This is one of the most well-known Korean expressions, especially among K-pop fans. 보고 싶다 literally = "want to see" but is the standard way to say "I miss you" in Korean. There is no separate word for "miss" in the English sense — Korean expresses it through wanting to see.

--- word ---
term: 싶더라고
type: auxiliary adjective + retrospective ending
breakdown: 싶다 (to want to) → 싶 (stem) + 더라고 (retrospective casual ending)
meaning: I realized I wanted to / I found that I wanted to
notes: Two grammar layers here. First, -고 싶다 = "to want to (do something)." 보고 싶다 = want to see. The -더- infix is key — it signals the speaker is reflecting on a past feeling or discovery. Learners often find -더- difficult because it does not exist in English — think of it as the speaker stepping back and reporting on their own experience as if observing it from the outside.
